Research

We study how individual differences in cognition develop, how social, ecological, and anthropogenic factors shape animal cognition in the wild, and how cognition affects fitness outcomes. Cities are among the fastest-changing environments on Earth, so we use urbanization as a natural experiment to ask how cognitive abilities develop, evolve, and help animals adapt to living alongside people.

Research themes

Self-domestication

HyenasCoyotes

Animals living near people may face selection for less fear and aggression toward humans — the same pressures thought to have shaped early domestication. We ask whether urban hyenas and coyotes show signs of this “self-domestication” in their morphology, cognition, and prosocial behavior, and what it means for how they interact with people.

Problem-solving

HyenasCoyotes

We develop a wide array of cognitive apparatus, from puzzle boxes like the multi-access box to tasks built for the field, to test abilities such as innovation, inhibitory control, and cooperation in wild animals. We then ask how individual traits, social factors, and urban living shape these abilities, and whether better problem-solvers gain a fitness advantage.

Responses to novelty

Coyotes

How an animal reacts to something new — approaching it or avoiding it — shapes whether it can take advantage of new opportunities, and how likely it is to come into conflict with people. We study neophobia and exploration in coyotes and how these responses relate to life in human-dominated landscapes.

Communication

Coyotes

Vocal communication coordinates group life, from deciding where to move to keeping track of who is nearby. Building on our work with meerkat “move” calls, we are investigating coyote vocal communication and how it may change in human-dominated environments.

Study systems

Mara Hyena Project

Hyenas

The Mara Hyena Project has studied wild spotted hyenas in the Maasai Mara National Reserve, Kenya, since 1988, following individually known hyenas across clans and generations. This long-term record lets us link cognition to development, social rank, and lifetime reproductive success.

Our hyena work has also extended to urban hyenas in Harar and Mekelle, Ethiopia, where hyenas live alongside people — a rare opportunity to compare the cognition of the same species in wild and urban settings.

Coyote Project

Coyotes

Coyotes are one of North America’s great urban success stories, now living in cities from Los Angeles to New York. Their flexibility makes them an ideal species for asking how cognition helps animals cope with city life. Our coyote research focuses on responses to novelty, problem-solving, vocal communication, and self-domestication, in collaboration with the Gotham Coyote Project.

Previous work: meerkat communication

Meerkats

As a postdoc at the University of Zurich, Dr. Johnson-Ulrich studied meerkat communication and collective movement at the Kalahari Research Centre, South Africa, using playback experiments to test how “move” calls influence group decisions.
